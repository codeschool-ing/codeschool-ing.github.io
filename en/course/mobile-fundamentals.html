Course

Mobile Development Fundamentals

What is different about a program that lives in someone’s pocket: a lifecycle the system controls, a screen that changes size and orientation, permissions, a network that comes and goes, and a battery that is not yours to spend.

  • 60 hours
  • Level: beginner

What you need first

No mobile experience assumed. Some programming, in any language: the examples read code without teaching it here.

What you will learn

  • The landscape: native, multiplatform and web, and what each one costs
  • The app lifecycle, and the system that kills your process
  • Layouts that survive every screen and both orientations
  • Navigation: stacks, tabs and deep links
  • Permissions, privacy and the data on the device
  • Design guidelines, accessibility and store review

Full topic list

  • Android and iOS: the two platforms, and what is genuinely different between them
  • Native, multiplatform (React Native, Flutter, Kotlin Multiplatform) and web wrappers
  • Choosing an approach: team, performance, device features and time
  • The app lifecycle: foreground, background, suspended, killed
  • Saving and restoring state when the system takes the process away
  • The main thread, jank, and the 16ms budget for a frame
  • Screen density: points, dp, and the asset in three sizes
  • Layouts that stretch: phone, tablet, foldable and landscape
  • Safe areas, notches, keyboards and the bottom bar
  • Navigation: stack, tabs, drawer, and the back button that only Android has
  • Deep links, universal links, and opening the app from outside it
  • Permissions: asking at the right moment, and the refusal that is final
  • Privacy: what the store makes you declare, and the data not worth collecting
  • Connectivity: offline, slow networks, and the request that never returns
  • Battery, background work, and what the system will not let you run
  • Storage on the device: files, preferences and the sandbox
  • Push notifications: how they arrive, and why they are not a channel to abuse
  • Material Design and the Human Interface Guidelines, side by side
  • Accessibility on a touchscreen: labels, contrast, target size and screen readers
  • The stores: review, rejection, versioning, and what changes after publishing
